A number worth checking

Is London really cycling 1.66 million times a day?

Transport for London says daily cycle journeys reached 1.66 million in 2026, four years ahead of target. Nobody counted 1.66 million bicycles. This page shows how the figure is produced, which parts of the chain we can rebuild from TfL's open data, and which parts we cannot.

Short answer: plausible, but not verifiable yet
1.66million daily cycle journey stages, 2026
(TfL, Travel in London, 25 Nov 2026)

1 · The claim

What exactly is being claimed

The ianVisits article repeats TfL's press release: daily cycle journeys rose 10.1% in 2026, from 1.50 million to 1.66 million, passing the 1.6 million target set for 2030.

Two details matter before we look at any data:

  • "Journeys" means journey stages. A stage is one leg by one mode. Cycling to a station, taking the train, then walking contains one cycle stage. The headline target counts stages. Counted as whole trips, TfL gives 1.55 million for 2026 (1.41 million for 2025).
  • It is a modelled average day, not a count. It is a seven-day average built from weekday spring counts and a household travel survey. The result is quoted to three significant digits, but, as we will see, that precision is not earned.

2 · How the number is built

From a camera on a street corner to "1.66 million"

TfL publishes a methodology note that is refreshingly explicit. The chain has six links. Some inputs are public, some are not.

  1. Count at a fixed panel of sites public data

    1,451 count locations were drawn to represent every kind of road and path where cycling is allowed. Each is counted once a year, in spring, on a "neutral" weekday (preferably Tue–Thu, no holiday or strike) from 06:00 to 22:00, in both directions, mostly from video that is then reviewed by hand. Per site, all 15-minute counts are summed:

    qs = Σ cycles counted at site s, 06:00–22:00, all directions

    In the 2025 files the average panel site recorded 1,398 cycles that day, but the median site only 509: a few central roads carry enormous flows.

  2. Split the panel into 15 strata public data

    3 areas (central = inside the Congestion Charge zone, 209 sites; inner, 748; outer, 494) × 5 road types (A road, B road, minor road, local street, motor-vehicle-free path). Within each stratum the site flows are averaged:

    q̄g = (1 / Ng) · Σ qs for g = 1 … 15

  3. Scale up with network length to get weekday cycle-km lengths not published

    Flows cannot be added across sites (the same rider passes several), but distances can. Each stratum's average flow is multiplied by the total length of cyclable network in that stratum (roads plus paths, from GIS data), as if every counted cycle rode the stratum's average share of the network.

    cycle-kmweekday = Σg Lg · q̄g

    TfL does not publish the 15 lengths Lg. We estimated them from OpenStreetMap (table below).

  4. Convert kilometres into stages LTDS factors not published

    Cycle-km become stages by dividing by an average stage length from the London Travel Demand Survey (LTDS, about 16,000 residents a year): a three-year average of straight-line stage lengths on Mon–Thu, excluding stages over 20 km, multiplied by a "wiggle" factor of 1.3 because real routes are longer than the crow flies. Weekend riding is not counted at all; a weekday-to-weekend ratio from the same survey adds it back.

    stageswd = cycle-km / Swd Swd = 1.3 × mean crow-fly stage length (Mon–Thu) stageswe = cycle-km / Swe × f f = weekend stages ÷ weekday stages (LTDS) stages = (5 · stageswd + 2 · stageswe) / 7 ← the 1.66 million

  5. Convert stages to trips LTDS factors not published

    Trips = stages × (trips ÷ stages ratio from the LTDS), computed separately for weekdays and weekends, then the same 5:2 weighting. This gives the 1.55 million trips.

  6. Repeat every year, compare with the previous one partly open

    TfL stresses that the series is designed for relative change; the absolute levels carry uncertainty. 2020 and 2021 are excluded because of the pandemic.

Reading the chain as one line

Because steps 4 and 5 are just constants, the headline is effectively:

stages ≈ κ · Σg Lg · q̄g where κ = (5/Swd + 2f/Swe) / 7

The open counts give us q̄g for every year. TfL's published stage totals then tell us what κ must have been. Whether κ stays constant is the key question below.

4 · Cycling over time

What the counts say about the trend

The open spring counts go back to 2015. For every year we computed the weekday cycle-km index (step 3) and scaled it with a single constant so that 2025 matches TfL's published 1.50 million. The hollow line is therefore what the counts alone imply if the conversion factor κ never changed.

Three phases are visible. Between 2015 and 2019 our counts-based line rises about 11%, while TfL's published stage series is down 3% over the same years (it implies about 1.08 million in 2015). So even before the pandemic the survey-based conversion step moved the published series by roughly 13% relative to the raw counts. The pandemic then reset behaviour. Since 2022 panel flows have risen every year, with the biggest jump in the 2025 wave.

Weather matters

The counts are one day per site. Rain suppresses cycling. In the 2025 wave only 5.7% of recorded 15-minute slots were logged as wet, against 14.6% in 2024. TfL itself notes that spring 2025 "had particularly good weather". That makes 2025 a high base for the 2026 comparison.

5 · Can 1.66 million be checked?

Seven tests, three outcomes

TestResultEvidence
The figure was really publishedYESTfL press release, 25 Nov 2026: 1.66 m stages (+10.1%), 1.55 m trips. The ianVisits article reports it correctly, except that it says "journeys" where TfL means stages.
The numbers agree with each otherYES1.66 ÷ 1.05 (2019) = 1.58, matching "+57.7%". 1.66 ÷ 1.101 = 1.508, so 2025 appears to have been revised slightly from 1.496 to about 1.51 (the 2025 area figures summed to 1.496 m). Naive 1.66 ÷ 1.50 would be +10.7%, which is rounding. The trips-to-stages ratio is 0.934 in 2026 and 0.94 in 2025.
The method is sound and documentedYESStratified sample, counts → cycle-km → stages. Every step is described in TfL's note, and we rebuilt steps 1–3 from public data.
Panel counts really rose since 2019YESOn 1,438 sites present in both waves, stratum-weighted flow rose +32.5% (95% CI +28% to +37%) from 2019 to 2025 and +9.6% (+7.0% to +11.7%) from 2024 to 2025.
The 2025 level of 1.50 millionPARTLYWe reproduce the trend and the area split, but not the absolute level: it needs TfL's stratum lengths and LTDS stage lengths. Our OSM-based network (20,300 km) implies about 6 km per stage; scaling the network to TfL's own hint (431 km of Cycleways = 2.5% of all cyclable roads, so ~17,200 km) implies about 5.3 km. Both numbers are consistent with the published 1.50 million by construction, but we cannot tell which is closer to TfL's own inputs, because TfL does not publish its stage lengths. The spread shows how much the closed inputs matter.
Independent survey agrees on level and trendNOThe LTDS puts London residents' cycling at about 0.06 trips per person per day, "similar" to before the pandemic (+1% from 2019 to 2024). That is roughly 0.55 million trips a day for 9.1 million residents, against 1.4 million estimated from counts, and a flat trend against +42%. TfL lists reasons (visitors, commuters from outside London, couriers, survey sample size, different time windows) but cannot separate them.
The 2026 value itselfNOT YETThe open archive ends at the spring/autumn 2025 waves. The spring 2026 counts, the 2026 LTDS factors and the stratum lengths are not public, so 1.66 million cannot be recomputed. We can only ask what it would take.

What would spring 2026 have to look like?

Hold the stage-per-cycle-km factor κ constant, and +10.1% in stages needs +10.1% in weekday cycle-km. But κ has not been constant: between 2024 and 2025 stages grew 12.7% while cycle-km grew about 9.1%, so κ rose 3.3%. If it drifts again, counts only need to rise about 6.6%.

How precise can the number be?

Resampling the 1,451 panel sites within each stratum (a bootstrap) gives a sampling error of about ±6% (95% interval) on the 2025 level, before any uncertainty in lengths, stage lengths or weekend ratios. The 2026 headline sits 3.8% above the 1.6 million target. That margin is smaller than the sampling noise on the level. The growth since 2019 is far better determined than the level, because the same sites are compared year to year.

6 · Perspective

How does London compare?

City comparisons are fragile: different boundaries, different definitions of "trip", different survey years. The figures below come from each city's own sources and are indicative only. Greater London is far larger and less dense than Paris or Copenhagen proper, so the per-resident gap is somewhat overstated.

Two readings are possible. Even on TfL's headline, London is still well behind Paris, Berlin and Copenhagen per resident. And the spread between London's two own sources (0.06 versus 0.17 trips per resident per day) is as large as the gap to the leaders, which is another reason not to over-read three significant digits.

I looked for a primary-source figure for Amsterdam and Utrecht but did not find a comparable per-resident number I could verify, so I left the Dutch cities out rather than quote one from memory.

7 · Conclusion

Can 1.66 million be right?

As the output of TfL's method, yes. As an exact number of cycle stages made on an average London day, nobody can say, and the 2026 figure cannot yet be verified.

Here is why the number stands, and why it falls short of proof:

What holds upThe method is coherent. Counts really did rise: +32% on matched sites since 2019 and about +10% in 2025 alone. The published figures are internally consistent.
What we could not checkThe 2026 counts, the stratum lengths, the LTDS stage lengths and weekend factors. About a quarter of the post-2019 growth in the published series comes from those conversions, not from counts.
What argues for cautionA ±6% sampling error. A dry 2025 base. A resident survey that shows flat cycling at a much lower level. The target was cleared by just 3.8%.

Our verdict: plausible, directionally credible, not independently verifiable. A 10% rise needs counts to grow by roughly 7–10% in spring 2026, which is within what the 2025 wave showed. It is a reasonable estimate, in the sense that no step is obviously wrong. But "four years early" and "1.66 million" are statements of precision that the data behind them do not support. A fair description (our judgement) is "roughly 1.5 to 1.8 million daily cycle stages, with a clear upward trend".

When the spring 2026 wave is added to the open data folder, this page's pipeline can be re-run in a few minutes to settle the question.

What would improve the analysis

  • TfL could publish the 15 stratum lengths and the LTDS stage lengths and ratios. With those, the whole chain becomes reproducible.
  • Uncertainty ranges next to every headline number would settle the "target reached" argument either way.
  • Proper congestion-zone and inner-cordon polygons would replace our nearest-site assignment of OSM roads.
  • Use OS Highways (TfL's source) instead of OSM, and test other road-type mappings.
  • Add the Cycleway programme and automatic counters (Blackfriars, Embankment) as independent trend checks.
  • Add Santander Cycles hires and dockless data for a second opinion, and weather-adjust the spring counts.
  • A like-for-like comparison with Amsterdam, Utrecht and other cities using each city's own count programme.
